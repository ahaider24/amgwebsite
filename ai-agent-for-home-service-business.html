Everyone's selling "AI agents." Here's what one actually does all day.

The phrase means nothing until you watch one work. So here's a plain walkthrough of the agents we run for home service businesses. What they touch, what they never touch, and how to tell what's real when somebody tries to sell you one.

A
Amir Haider Founder, AmirGets · August 2026 · 4 min read

Strip the mystery out of the word first. An AI agent is software that notices something happened in your business, does the same careful steps a good office manager would do, and then stops and asks a person at exactly the moments a person should decide. That's it. Not a robot employee. Not a chatbot bolted to your homepage. A set of chores, done on time, every time, with a log you can read.

Here's what that looks like on a real morning.

7:55 a.m. The reviews get read before you've had coffee.

A new Google review came in overnight. The agent sees it, reads what the customer actually said, and drafts a reply in the owner's voice. Not a template. The owner's actual style, learned from how he already talks to his customers: thank them by name, mention the job, keep it short. The draft waits for a yes. When it posts, it posts during business hours, because a review answered at 3 a.m. reads like a machine answered it.

And if the review is a one-star? The agent doesn't touch it. Ever. It flags the owner and steps aside, because an angry customer deserves a human and an automated apology makes everything worse. That rule is built in, not optional. That's the difference between an agent and a spam cannon.

2 p.m. A bid request hits the website.

Someone fills out the form: what they need lifted or fixed, where, when. Before they've closed the tab, the request is in the company's CRM as a customer record, and the owner's phone buzzes with the name, the number, the job, and the timing. Not an email he'll find tonight. A text he can act on from the truck, while the customer is still warm and still hasn't called the next name on the list.

Speed is most of the game in the trades. The first contractor who responds usually wins the job. An agent's whole advantage is that it never has its hands full.

What it never does.

It never decides what a job should cost. It never talks to an upset customer. It never sends work out the door. It never invents a fact about your business, because everything it says is drawn from things you actually said and did. The judgment stays with the person whose name is on the truck. The agent does the noticing, the remembering, and the paperwork, which is exactly the work that slips when the phone is ringing and the crew needs answers.

The owner does the work. The agent does the remembering.

How to tell if something should be an agent's job.

The test is simple. Does it happen every week? Does it follow the same steps each time? Is there a clear moment when it's done, and a way to check it worked? If yes, it's agent work. If it needs your gut, your pricing instinct, or your relationship with the customer, it stays yours. Most businesses have five or six chores that pass this test, and they're almost always the ones quietly leaking money: the review that never got answered, the lead that sat until Monday, the follow-up that never went out.

If someone's selling you one, ask these three questions.

One: show me the log. A real agent keeps a record of every single thing it did, and a vendor who can't show you one is selling a story. Two: what happens when it fails? The honest answer involves an alert to a human, not a promise that it won't. Three: who approves? Anything a customer will see should wait for a yes until the agent has earned trust, the same way you'd treat a new hire.
